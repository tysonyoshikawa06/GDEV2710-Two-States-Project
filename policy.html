Policy

This page explores some state constitutional language on K-12 education, relevant federal/state court cases, and applicable federal/state laws. It also compares these elements, later providing evidence and context based recommendations for each state.

Policy Elements

State Constitutional Language

Hawaii Constitution

The Hawaii State Constitution, in Article X, establishes a strong, centralized commitment to public education. It states that "The State shall provide for the establishment, support and control of a statewide system of public schools free from sectarian control," clearly identifying K–12 education as a direct responsibility tied to the state government. This language is significant because it places the entire weight of educational oversight and funding on the state rather than local or independent school districts. As a result, Hawaii is the only U.S. state that operates its public education system as a single, statewide school district, enabling a high degree of uniformity and equity in its schools.

A key section is Section 4 of Article X, which requires the state to offer a Hawaiian education program that includes instruction in Hawaiian language, culture, and history. This constitutional mandate is both culturally and legally important: it ensures that Native Hawaiian identity is not just recognized but actively integrated into the public education system. This sets Hawaii apart from many other states whose constitutions make no such commitment to indigenous education or cultural preservation.

However, despite this specificity, the Hawaii Constitution makes no mention of student-to-teacher ratios or class size requirements. This omission is important, as it means that staffing levels and class sizes are determined entirely by policy decisions, bargaining agreements, or budget constraints; not by constitutional mandate. While this gives the state Department of Education flexibility to allocate resources where needed, it also means that there is no constitutional guarantee that students get a minimum level of individual attention.

This absence could be seen as a structural weakness in the constitution. A constitutional reference to student-teacher ratios, even as a mere recommendation, might strengthen efforts to ensure quality education across the islands, especially in under-resourced rural areas. Still, Hawaii’s centralized system means that this isn't a large issue, and that it can get away with this lack of specificity.

California Constitution

The California State Constitution, through Article IX, lays the legal foundation for public K–12 education, establishing it as a direct responsibility of the state. The most important section is Section 5. It declares that “The Legislature shall provide for a system of common schools by which a free school shall be kept up and supported in each district at least six months in every year.” This language ensures that every child in California is guaranteed access to a free public education, and it places this obligation in the hands of State Legislature.

An important section is Section 2, which establishes an elected Superintendent of Public Instruction as the executive officer of the California Department of Education. This holds California’s top education official accountable to voters. This position caries heavy administrative weight, and provides some centralization to the California education system.

However, while the Constitution protects access and guarantees free schooling, it has no mention about student to teacher ratios or class sizes. This absence is significant–without a constitutional mandate around ratios, California’s class sizes and staffing levels are left in the air, fluctuating year by year. This lack of specificity has implications for educational equity, especially in low-income or rural districts where class sizes tend to be larger and resources tend to be more limited.

Federal/State Court Cases

Hawaii Court Cases

Felix v. Lingle (1993)

This case impacted special education staffing in Hawaii. Over 13,000 children with disabilities alleged the state was failing to provide the educational and mental health services guaranteed by the federal Individuals with Disabilities Education Act and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act In 1994, U.S. District Judge David Ezra found Hawaii liable under these federal laws for its educational deficiencies. A mandate followed, requiring the Department of Education to dramatically expand and improve services for students with special needs. A key component was addressing the severe teacher shortages. Hawaii was placed under court orders to hire hundreds of additional special education teachers to meet mandated student to teacher ratios.

California Court Cases

Serrano v. Priest (1976)

This case established education as a fundamental right under the California constitution. The California Supreme Court stopped the state’s property-tax-based funding system, which had led to “glaring inter-district revenue inequities” and educational disparities. The court held that a child’s educational opportunity could not depend on district wealth and required the state to equalize school funding. Serrano thus paved the way for California’s school finance reforms to reduce class-size and resource gaps between rich and poor districts.

Butt v. State of California (1992)

This case extended Serrano’s equal protection principles. When a California school district (Richmond) shortened its school year by six weeks to cut costs, the California Supreme Court intervened, ruling that the state had a constitutional obligation to prevent such a drastic reduction in instruction for one group of students. The Court found that Richmond’s early closure would have denied those students their fundamental right to basic educational equality. Butt affirmed that even an increased student–teacher ratio due to saving money by cutting instructional days violated California’s constitution regarding education equity.

Applicable Federal/State Laws

Hawaii Laws

Policy 106-2 (2004)

This policy establishes the student-teacher ratio for Hawaii public schools. It mentions the optimal ratios for K-3 (20:1) and 4-12 (26:1). The policy states that the DOE is responsible for allocating staff and funds such that these ideals are met.

House Bill 1200 (2025)

House Bill 1200 seeks to formalize class size limits into state law, building upon existing guidelines. The bill proposes maximum class sizes of 25 students for kindergarten through 2nd grade, 20 students for 3rd grade, and 26 students for grades 4–12. It mandates that schools unable to meet these limits must notify parents, providing reasons for any deviations.

California Laws

Education Code Section 41376 (2023)

This policy outlines maximum class sizes for grades 1–3, setting an average limit of 30 students per class, with no single class exceeding 32 students. For grades 4–8, the average number of students per teacher must not exceed 29.9 or the district's average in 1964. Districts exceeding these limits will financial penalties.

Education Code Section 52055.740 (2023)

This section mandates specific class size reductions for certain schools. For kindergarten through 3rd grade, classes are limited to no more than 20 students. In grades 4–8, classrooms must have an average size that is either five students fewer than the 2006–07 average or capped at 25 students, whichever is less. For grades 4–12, subject-specific classes in core subjects must also adhere to similar limits.

Comparative Analysis and Recommendations

Hawaii and California represent two fundamentally different models of public education governance. While one is centralized, the other is heavily decentralized, and this difference shapes their approaches to student-teacher ratios, resource allocation, and educational equity. Hawaii benefits from centralized decision making and uniform policies. Its constitution clearly states the responsibility for public education to the state, allowing for consistent support across the system. While no mentions of specific student to teacher ratios are in the state constitution, policies were made to set clear student–teacher ratio guidelines. Through this and the centralized system, Hawaii has been able to maintain a low student-teacher ratio and decently equitable educational opportunity across scools.

California, in contrast, operates with over 1,000 local school districts. This has led to wide disparities in class sizes and teacher distribution, especially in underserved communities. Court cases such as Serrano v. Priest and Butt v. State of California were key in pushing the state to reduce funding inequities and prevent extreme educational disruptions. However, even with certain policies like dedicated to outlining specific student-teacher ratio regulations, California still struggles to consistently enforce small class sizes across districts.

To improve equity and outcomes, California should consider expanding centralized oversight for staffing allocation, especially in high-need districts. A more centralized system would allow for more uniform policy and easier resource allocation, evening student-teacher ratios across districts and reducing inequality for less affluent areas.

Meanwhile, Hawaii could strengthen its approach by defining clear class size protections in its constitution, giving more legal weight to existing policies and safeguarding them from fluctuating circumstances and new policies. Both states would also benefit from greater investment in their teachers, especially in rural and underprivileged areas where staffing shortages contribute to high student to teacher ratios.

Ultimately, while both states deal with differing circumstances and challenges both models can evolve by borrowing strengths from each other. Hawaii providing more legal power to their educational policies, and California adopting more uniform standards and structures to protect its most vulnerable students.
